Picture books for naming feelings with toddlers

Use picture books, playful questions, and a free sample to explore feelings words with your toddler.

Fursay Family Learning Editorial Team · Published: 2026-11-14 · Updated: 2026-11-14

Start with a picture, not a test

A picture book gives you and your toddler something to notice together. Choose a moment when you both have room to look, perhaps after a snack or during floor play. Open to one image and describe a detail: a face, a posture, or something happening nearby. Then offer a possible feeling word, such as happy, sad, worried, or excited. You do not need to cover a list or make your child repeat the word. One shared observation can be enough for today.

Keep your guess flexible. “She might feel worried” leaves space for a toddler to point somewhere else or suggest angry. Ask what they noticed if they want to tell you, but let a gesture or quiet look count too. Faces in illustrations can be interpreted in more than one way. The aim is to give your family words for conversation, not to find a single correct answer or decide what your child feels.

Explore contrasts in The Feelings Book

With The Feelings Book, choose a picture that catches your toddler's attention and name the feeling you think it suggests. Invite your child to copy a face or make a movement, if that sounds fun. You might say, “This looks like a big excited feeling. What could excited feet do?” Let the child show you, then describe the movement together. If copying faces is uncomfortable, a toy can take the turn instead.

On another reading, choose a different feeling and compare it with the first. Ask whether the toy wants to jump, sit quietly, or do something else. Both feelings can belong in the same day, and there is no need to sort them into good and bad. If your toddler turns the page before answering, follow along. A short comment from you can model a word without making the book into a quiz.

Make room for possibilities in In My Heart

In My Heart offers another opening for talking about feelings. Pause at an image your child chooses and wonder aloud: “I wonder what this feeling is like.” Your toddler might respond with a color, a sound, or a word from another language. Repeat their contribution and add a possible feeling word only if it fits the conversation. You can look again another day and hear a completely different idea.

Connect the imagined feeling to a simple situation without assuming the picture tells you everything. “Maybe someone is excited because a friend is coming.” Then ask what the character might want to do. A child may imagine dancing, finding a quiet place, or staying close to someone. Accept the idea rather than correcting the story to match an adult's expectation. The discussion can end with the picture; a toddler does not have to disclose a personal experience.

Move from a character to everyday words

After reading, use a toy to make a tiny scene: a bear cannot find its blanket, then finds it under a cushion. Say, “The bear might be worried. Now it looks relieved.” Pause between the two moments so your toddler can move the toy or change the ending. You can use familiar words from your home language alongside English, choosing whichever expression makes the situation easiest to understand.

Later, model a feeling of your own in a small, ordinary moment: “I feel disappointed that the rain stopped our walk. Let's choose something to do inside.” This shows a word, a reason, and a possible next step. Avoid telling your child what they must be feeling. If they offer a different description of their own experience, listen and use their words. The same event can bring several feelings at once.

Try the routine without buying a book

Neither title is necessary for this activity. Open the free Koko printable sample below, borrow a picture book from a library, or draw two simple faces on scrap paper. Look at one picture together and notice a detail before suggesting a feeling. Ask what a character might need, then let your toddler point, move a toy, or turn away. The free sample works on a screen too, so printing is optional.

A family photograph or a toy can also become a prompt, as long as everyone is comfortable with it. Make up a short situation rather than asking your toddler to explain a real person's private feelings. You might put a toy near a pretend puddle and wonder whether it feels curious or cautious. Stop when the game has run its course. There is no purchase, special material, or finished worksheet required.

Respond to the child in front of you

A toddler may enjoy naming feelings one day and prefer turning pages quickly the next. Watch for signs of interest, fatigue, or discomfort and adjust the activity. Silence, pointing, and making a sound are all ways to participate. You can close the book and return to a favorite page later; remembering a word on demand is not the measure of a successful reading time.

When your child is already upset, attend to their immediate need for comfort, space, or safety before attempting a story activity. A calm presence and listening matter more than finding the perfect label. Books can offer language for a later conversation, but they cannot diagnose a child's feelings or replace individual support. If distress persists or you have concerns about your child's wellbeing, consider speaking with a qualified local professional who knows your family's circumstances.
